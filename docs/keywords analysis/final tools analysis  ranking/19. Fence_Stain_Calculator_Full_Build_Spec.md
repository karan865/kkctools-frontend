### Step-by-Step Calculation Guide
## How Much Stain for a Fence? (Coverage & Gallons Formula)

To accurately estimate your exterior wood stain by hand before visiting the paint store or ordering online, follow this comprehensive 5-step mathematical process:

### 1. Calculate Single-Side Flat Surface Area
Measure the total running length of your fence line in linear feet and determine the vertical height in feet. Multiply length by height:
- Base Flat Area (sq ft) = Fence Length (ft) × Fence Height (ft)  
*Example:* A standard 150-foot-long privacy fence that stands 6 feet tall:  
- Base Flat Area = 150 ft × 6 ft = **900.0 Square Feet**

### 2. Account for One Side vs. Both Sides
Decide whether you are staining only the interior face facing your backyard or both the interior and exterior faces (or sharing the project with a neighbor):
- If staining **One Side Only:** Sides Multiplier = 1.0 (900.0 sq ft)
- If staining **Both Sides:** Sides Multiplier = 2.0 (900.0 × 2 = **1,800.0 Square Feet**)

### 3. Multiply by the Picket Style Overlap Factor
Because fence pickets have distinct thicknesses, gaps, and overlaps, flat square footage must be adjusted for actual 3-dimensional wooden surface area:
- **Standard Flat Privacy (Solid edge-to-edge):** 1.00 Multiplier
- **Board-on-Board (Overlapping vertical pickets):** **1.30 Multiplier** (overlapping faces and edges add 30% surface area)
- **Shadowbox (Alternating pickets on front and back):** **1.25 Multiplier** (adds 25% surface area)
- **Spaced Picket (Ornamental front-yard picket):** **0.80 Multiplier** (gaps reduce overall wood area by ~20%)
- **Split Rail / Ranch Fence (2 to 3 horizontal rails):** **0.40 Multiplier**

*Example (Both sides of a 150 ft flat privacy fence):*  
- Effective Stained Area = 1,800 sq ft × 1.0 = **1,800.0 Square Feet**

### 4. Calculate Gallons Based on Wood Porosity
Dividing your effective surface area by your wood's specific absorption coverage rate yields your base gallon requirement. Unfinished wood grain absorbs stain at drastically different rates:
- **Rough-Sawn Cedar or Unfinished Pine:** 175 Square Feet per Gallon
- **Weathered, Parched Dry Wood (Gray aged fence):** 150 Square Feet per Gallon (thirsty wood absorbs heavily)
- **Smooth-Planed Lumber or Previously Sealed Wood:** 225 Square Feet per Gallon

- Coat 1 Gallons = 1,800 sq ft / 175 sq ft per gal = **10.29 Gallons**

### Wood Porosity & Coverage Guide
## Wood Condition & Grain Absorption: Rough-Sawn vs. Weathered vs. Smooth

The single largest factor causing DIYers to run out of stain mid-project is misjudging wood grain porosity. Review this breakdown to determine your fence's true absorption category:

### 1. Rough-Sawn Unfinished Cedar & Pine (175 Sq Ft per Gallon)
- **Appearance & Feel:** Coarse, jagged, velvety texture directly from the lumber mill saw blades. You can feel rough wood splinters when rubbing your hand along the picket.
- **Absorption Profile:** Extreme capillary action. The rough wood fibers act like microscopic straws, drinking liquid stain deep into the core. While this uses more stain initially, it provides the deepest, longest-lasting protection against internal dry rot and insect boring.

### 2. Aged, Parched, Weathered Gray Wood (150 Sq Ft per Gallon)
- **Appearance & Feel:** Fences that have weathered unprotected for 3 to 10 years, turning silver-gray. The surface is often checking (fine hair-line cracks opening along the wood grain).
- **Absorption Profile:** Ultra-high absorption. Dried-out, sun-baked wood lignins have collapsed, leaving massive microscopic air voids. Weathered wood will greedily soak up stain almost as fast as you can brush it on. Always clean with an oxygenated wood bleach or brightener prior to staining to ensure uniform color intake.

### 3. Smooth-Planed or Previously Sealed Wood (225 Sq Ft per Gallon)
- **Appearance & Feel:** Factory-milled smooth pickets, dimensional lumber planed on four sides (S4S), or fences that were stained within the last 2 to 3 years and still exhibit mild water-beading.
- **Absorption Profile:** Controlled, moderate absorption. Stain spreads smoothly across the surface without immediate sinking, providing high square-foot coverage per gallon.

### Pro Contractor Staining Tips
## 6 Essential Pro Tips for Staining a Wood Fence Like a Master Painter

1. **Always "Box" Your Stain Pails Together:** Even when buying the exact same brand and color code, computer tint machines produce microscopic color variations between separate 1-gallon and 5-gallon containers. If you switch cans midway along a fence line, you may notice an obvious color shift. Prevent this by pouring multiple cans or pails into a large 5-gallon or 10-gallon tub and stirring thoroughly (a professional painter practice known as "boxing").
2. **Never Stain Fence Pickets in Direct Scorching Sunlight:** Staining a hot fence in 85°F+ direct sun causes volatile solvents and water to evaporate before the oil resins have time to penetrate the cellular grain of the wood. The stain will flash-dry on the surface, leaving lap marks, streaks, and premature surface flaking. Work in the shade, following the sun around your house, or stain on an overcast day.
3. **Always Back-Brush When Using an Airless Sprayer:** An airless paint sprayer is phenomenal for quickly coating hundreds of feet of fencing and shooting stain into narrow gaps. However, spraying alone leaves microscopic droplets floating on the wood surface. Always have a helper follow immediately behind the spray tip with a wide 4-inch or 5-inch exterior stain brush to push the wet stain deep into open wood grain pores and smooth out drips.
4. **Perform the "Water Drop Test" for Moisture:** Never apply oil stain to damp wood or newly installed pressure-treated lumber that is still saturated with chemical preservatives. Sprinkle several drops of water onto the fence pickets. If the water beads up into droplets, the wood pores are sealed or wet—wait 2 to 4 weeks. If the water immediately soaks into the wood within 10 seconds, the lumber is thirsty and ready for stain.
5. **Protect Ground Turf and Concrete Patios with Shield Boards:** When spraying or rolling near the ground, use a 3-foot piece of cardboard, corrugated plastic, or an aluminum paint shield pressed against the bottom of the pickets. This keeps wet stain off lawn turf and prevents unsightly overspray stains on adjacent concrete patios, sidewalks, and foundation walls.
6. **Work Top-to-Bottom, Two Pickets at a Time:** When brushing or rolling, stain the top of the picket first and work your way down to the ground. Maintain a "wet edge" by completing 2 or 3 pickets completely from top to bottom before moving to the next section. Never stop staining in the middle of a picket or rail, as the overlap will leave a permanent dark lap stripe when dried.

### Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)
## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many gallons of stain do I need for a 150-foot fence?
A standard 6-foot-tall, 150-foot wooden privacy fence has 900 sq ft of surface area per side (1,800 sq ft total for both sides). On rough-sawn cedar absorbing stain at 175 sq ft per gallon, one coat on both sides requires approximately **10 to 11 gallons**. For two coats with a 10% waste buffer, order approximately **20 gallons (or four 5-gallon contractor pails)**.

### How many square feet does 1 gallon of fence stain cover?
On smooth, previously sealed wood, 1 gallon of exterior stain covers approximately **200 to 250 square feet**. On rough-sawn, unsealed cedar or aged weathered pine, coverage drops to **150 to 175 square feet per gallon** due to heavy capillary wood grain absorption.

### Does a board-on-board fence take more stain?
**Yes, significantly more.** A board-on-board fence features overlapping vertical pickets, creating approximately **30% more surface area and edge exposure** than a flat privacy fence. Always multiply flat fence square footage by **1.30** when estimating stain for board-on-board designs.

### Should I apply one coat or two coats of fence stain?
For semi-transparent or transparent penetrating oil stains on new or bare wood, **2 coats are strongly recommended** to provide maximum UV protection, mold resistance, and long-term weatherproofing. For solid color acrylic stains or routine maintenance refreshing where color is already established, **1 uniform coat** is usually sufficient.

### How often should a wood fence be stained?
A wooden fence should typically be restained every **3 to 5 years** when using a quality semi-transparent penetrating oil stain, or every **1 to 2 years** for clear water-repellent sealers. Solid color acrylic stains can last **5 to 7 years** before requiring maintenance.

### What is the difference between fence stain and deck stain?
While many modern exterior wood coatings can be used on both surfaces, **deck stains are formulated for heavy horizontal foot traffic, standing water puddles, and abrasion resistance**. Fence stains, on the other hand, are engineered specifically for vertical surfaces, focusing on deep pigment penetration, anti-sag viscosity, and maximum UV solar reflectivity.

### Can I spray fence stain instead of brushing?
Yes, using an airless paint sprayer (with a 513 or 515 spray tip) or a high-volume low-pressure (HVLP) unit speeds up fence staining by up to 400%. However, you must always **back-brush** wet stain into the wood pores immediately after spraying to eliminate runs, drips, and surface flashing. Always add a **15% overspray buffer** when using a sprayer.

### How long must new pressure-treated wood dry before staining?
Newly installed pressure-treated lumber is chemically saturated with water and copper-based preservatives. Depending on climate and sun exposure, you must typically allow new pressure-treated wood to dry for **2 to 4 months** before applying an oil-based stain. Test the wood by sprinkling water droplets: if they soak in within 10 seconds, the wood is dry enough to stain.
