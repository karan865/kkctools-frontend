### Step-by-Step Calculation Guide
## How Much Paver Base Do I Need? (Formula & Compaction Loss)

To accurately calculate your subbase aggregate by hand or verify material quotes from landscape supply yards, follow this comprehensive 5-step engineering formula:

### 1. Measure the Excavation Surface Area (Including Perimeter Overdig)
Start by measuring the planned exterior length and width of your finished patio, walkway, or driveway in feet. 

**The Essential 6-Inch Overdig Rule:** Concrete pavers require rigid plastic or aluminum edge restraints anchored with 10-inch steel spikes to prevent outward horizontal shifting. You cannot hammer a structural spike into soft topsoil or garden mulch. Therefore, you must excavate your crushed stone foundation **6 inches wider on all open perimeter edges** than the finished paver layout.

For example, if you plan a rectangular dining patio measuring 20 feet long by 15 feet wide with open turf on all four sides:
- Excavation Length = 20 ft + 0.5 ft (left) + 0.5 ft (right) = 21.0 feet
- Excavation Width = 15 ft + 0.5 ft (top) + 0.5 ft (bottom) = 16.0 feet
- Total Excavation Surface Area = 21.0 ft × 16.0 ft = **336.0 Square Feet**

If calculating an irregular curvilinear patio or composite multi-section courtyard, sum the square footage of each geometric zone and add a 5% to 10% perimeter overdig allowance.

### 2. Determine Finished Compacted Base Depth
Select your required base depth based on the planned load and existing soil conditions. Convert this depth from inches into fractional feet by dividing by 12:
- Standard Pedestrian Patio or Garden Walkway: 4 inches = 4 / 12 = **0.3333 Feet**
- Heavy Residential Patio (Hot Tub, Fire Pit, Outdoor Kitchen): 6 inches = 6 / 12 = **0.5000 Feet**
- Passenger Vehicle Driveway or RV Parking Pad: 8 to 10 inches = 8 / 12 = **0.6667 Feet**

### 3. Calculate Finished Compacted Volume in Cubic Feet
Multiply your total excavation area by the required base depth in feet:
- Compacted Cubic Feet = 336.0 sq ft × 0.3333 ft = **112.0 Cubic Feet**

### 4. Multiply by the Compaction Shrinkage Factor
Because plate compactors consolidate aggregate by forcing fine particles into open voids, you must increase your order volume. For standard plate tampers on loam or well-drained soil, use a **15% compaction allowance (1.15 multiplier)**. For heavy expansive clay soil or deep vehicular bases, use a **20% compaction allowance (1.20 multiplier)**:
- Loose Volume to Order = 112.0 cu ft × 1.15 = **128.8 Loose Cubic Feet**

### 5. Convert to Bulk Cubic Yards and US Short Tons
Commercial quarries and landscape bulk yards sell aggregate either by the cubic yard (volume) or by the ton (weight). Because 1 cubic yard equals exactly 27 cubic feet:
- Total Loose Cubic Yards = 128.8 cu ft / 27 = **4.77 Cubic Yards**

Crushed stone base (dense-graded limestone or crusher run containing stone dust) has a compacted-to-loose delivered density averaging **1.40 US Short Tons per cubic yard** (approximately 2,800 lbs/yd³ or 104 lbs/cu ft):
- Total Weight in US Short Tons = 4.77 cu yds × 1.40 tons/yd³ = **6.68 US Short Tons** (13,360 lbs)

**Quarry Ordering Rule:** Quarries rarely deliver partial fractions of a ton. Always round up to the nearest half-ton or full ton: place an order for **7.0 Tons**.

### Material Comparison: What Goes Under Pavers?
## Crushed Stone vs. Paver Base: What Material is Best?

Homeowners and DIY hardscapers frequently ask: *"What exact material should I ask for when calling the local quarry or gravel yard?"* Aggregate naming conventions vary significantly across North America. Below is a breakdown of the three primary aggregate categories used in hardscaping:

### 1. Dense-Graded Aggregate (Crusher Run / #21A / ABC Stone / DGA) — THE GOLD STANDARD
- **Regional Aliases:** Crusher run, dense graded aggregate (DGA), #21A limestone, crusher dust blend, quarry process (QP), shoulder stone, or road base.
- **Particle Composition:** A precision-engineered blend ranging from 3/4-inch jagged, fractured rock fragments down to coarse grit, fine silica particles, and pulverized mineral rock flour.
- **Why It Outperforms Everything Else:** When spread damp and compressed with a mechanical vibratory tamper, the fine stone particles wash into the spaces between the angular 3/4-inch stones. The jagged edges lock mechanically like puzzle pieces while the mineral dust binds under moisture, creating a concrete-like, impermeable subterranean foundation. It distributes heavy surface loads across a broad footprint and prevents pavers from shifting or sinking.

### 2. Recycled Crushed Concrete Aggregate (RCA / Crushed Concrete Base) — BEST VALUE
- **Regional Aliases:** 21AA crushed concrete, recycled road base, crushed miscellaneous base (CMB).
- **Particle Composition:** Clean demolished commercial concrete crushed and sieved to 3/4-inch minus with concrete dust fines.
- **Why Contractors Love It:** RCA offers structural compaction and sheer load characteristics virtually identical to virgin limestone, but typically costs **20% to 35% less per ton**. Furthermore, residual unhydrated cement particles in the concrete dust can slowly cure over time, creating an exceptionally stiff subbase that is ideal for clay soils and driveways.

### 3. Clean Open-Graded Stone (#57 Crushed Stone) — SPECIALIZED PERMEABLE APPLICATIONS ONLY
- **Regional Aliases:** #57 stone, 3/4-inch clean washed gravel, drainage stone.
- **Particle Composition:** Uniform 1/2-inch to 3/4-inch angular rocks washed completely free of sand, silt, and stone dust fines.
- **When to Use:** Standard interlocking pavers should **NEVER** be installed over clean stone without an engineering design. Because clean stone lacks fine bonding dust, it remains open and free-flowing. While exceptional for French drains and permeable green hardscaping that filters rainwater into stormwater aquifers, it requires a geotextile grid and specialized chip-stone bedding rather than standard concrete sand.

### Pro Contractor Compaction Tips
## 6 Essential Pro Tips for Compacting Paver Base Like a Master Hardscaper

1. **Always Compact Aggregate in 2-Inch to 3-Inch Lifts:** Never dump 6 inches of loose crusher run into an excavation hole and expect a plate compactor to consolidate it in a single pass. Standard commercial rental plate compactors (delivering 3,000 to 4,500 lbs of centrifugal force) only penetrate effectively to a depth of 2.5 to 3 inches. The bottom layer will remain loose, creating severe settling and sunken ruts within two years. Spread 2 to 3 inches of stone, compact thoroughly, then add the next lift.
2. **Moisten the Stone Before Running the Plate Compactor:** Dry aggregate creates excessive friction and clouds of choking mineral dust. Lightly spray your gravel bed with a garden hose until it is uniformly damp (like moist brownie mix or a wrung-out sponge). Water acts as a microscopic hydraulic lubricant, allowing angular stones to glide past one another and pack into zero-void density.
3. **Always Overdig the Perimeter by 6 Inches:** A paver patio is only as strong as its edges. Edge restraint systems (commercial plastic or aluminum snap-edge profiles) require 10-inch steel spikes driven vertically every 12 to 18 inches. If your compacted stone stops right at the paver edge, your spikes will drive into soft garden dirt, allowing the outer perimeter row of pavers to roll over and separate.
4. **Lay Heavy Geotextile Stabilization Fabric Over Native Subgrade:** Before spreading your first wheelbarrow of aggregate, lay a durable woven or non-woven geotextile filter fabric directly over the raw excavated subsoil. On silt or expansive clay soils, moisture causes sharp base rocks to punch downward into the mud while squishy mud pumps upward into your clean stone base. Geotextile fabric permanently separates these layers, dramatically extending the lifespan of your patio.
5. **Establish a Proper Slope for Drainage (1 Inch per 8 Feet):** Never build a perfectly flat outdoor hardscape. Standing water will seep into bedding sand and freeze in winter. Always pitch your subbase and finished surface away from house foundations, retaining walls, or basement entries at a minimum downward grade of 1 inch for every 8 to 10 linear feet (a 1% to 1.5% slope).
6. **Double-Check Base Smoothness with an 8-Foot Screed Board:** Before laying your 1-inch concrete sand bedding layer, place an 8-foot or 10-foot straight aluminum screed board across your compacted gravel. There should be no high ridges or deep birdbath hollows exceeding 1/4 inch. Your bedding sand should remain a perfectly uniform 1-inch cushion; never use sand to fill low depressions in an uneven gravel base.

### Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)
## Frequently Asked Questions

### How deep should a paver base be?
For standard pedestrian foot traffic such as backyard dining patios, garden walkways, and step landings, the universal standard is **4 inches of compacted dense-graded crushed stone**. For heavy applications including outdoor kitchens, stone masonry fire pits, hot tub pads, or residential car driveways, increase your crushed stone base depth to **6 to 8 inches**. For commercial vehicular parking or motorhome pads, ICPI standards specify **10 to 12 inches** of compacted aggregate.

### What is the best material to use for a paver base?
The industry standard and most widely recommended material is **dense-graded aggregate (DGA)**, commonly sold under regional names such as **crusher run, road base, #21A limestone, ABC stone, or quarry process**. This aggregate consists of jagged 3/4-inch crushed stone blended with smaller rock chips, coarse grit, and fine limestone rock dust. When vibrated with moisture, the fine dust locks the angular stones into an unyielding, concrete-like foundation that resists frost heave.

### Why do I need to add 15% to 20% for compaction shrinkage?
When loose crushed stone aggregate is spread across a project site, it contains significant air voids between particles. When run over multiple times with a heavy, gas-powered vibratory plate tamper, these air voids collapse and the stone fragments nest tightly together. This physical consolidation shrinks the total loose volume by **15% to 20%**. If you do not account for compaction loss when placing your quarry order, your finished base will be roughly one inch thinner than required.

### How many bags of paver base equal one cubic yard?
Standard retail paver base bags sold at home improvement warehouses contain 0.5 cubic feet of aggregate and weigh approximately 50 pounds. Because one bulk cubic yard contains exactly 27 cubic feet, you will need **54 individual retail bags** to equal one cubic yard of volume. Because of this high ratio, bagged aggregate is four to five times more expensive than ordering bulk delivery from a local landscape yard.

### Can I use sand as a paver base instead of gravel?
**No. You should never use sand as your primary paver base.** Sand particles are fluid and easily wash out or shift horizontally when saturated with heavy rain or subjected to foot traffic. Building a patio over 4 inches of sand will cause pavers to become wavy, uneven, and hazardous within months. Sand is strictly intended for a uniform, 1-inch bedding cushion placed on top of a solid 4-inch to 8-inch compacted gravel base.

### What is the difference between paver base and paver sand?
Paver base consists of 4 to 8 inches of 3/4-inch crushed gravel mixed with rock dust, designed to provide structural load-bearing capacity and frost protection. Paver sand (specifically ASTM C33 washed concrete sand) is a thin, 1-inch screeded cushion placed directly on top of the base to provide a smooth, workable setting bed for the pavers. After the pavers are placed, polymeric joint sand is swept between paver gaps to lock them firmly in place.

### How much does a ton of paver base cost?
At local commercial quarries and landscape supply yards, bulk crusher run or #21A paver base typically costs between **$30 and $55 per ton**, plus a local delivery dump fee ranging from $50 to $90. In bagged form at retail box stores, the same material costs approximately $160 to $220 per ton ($4.50 to $6.00 per 50 lb bag).

### Do I need to compact paver base in layers (lifts)?
Yes, absolutely. You must compact your crushed stone base in **layers no deeper than 2 to 3 inches at a time (known as lifts)**. Standard residential rental plate compactors only have sufficient centrifugal vibration force to penetrate 2 to 3 inches into loose aggregate. If you attempt to compact 6 inches of loose gravel all at once, only the top 2 inches will consolidate, while the bottom 4 inches will remain loose and settle over time.
